# Pattern Synthesis

Convert individual analyses into scoped, testable rules.

## Pattern Classes

- **Repeatable winning pattern** — positive evidence appears in at least three independent pieces across at least two accounts, with a reasonable baseline or comparison.
- **Repeatable losing pattern** — comparable content repeatedly underperforms or generates clear negative signals across at least three pieces and two accounts.
- **Emerging signal** — fewer examples support a plausible hypothesis that deserves testing.
- **Context-specific pattern** — evidence is strong but limited to one platform, persona, market, account, or format.
- **Contradictory signal** — examples point in different directions; preserve the disagreement.

Treat reused or reposted creative as one independent example unless the republishing context is the subject of analysis.

## Pattern Card

For every retained pattern, record:

| Field | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Pattern statement | Describe the behavior, not a vague theme |
| Class | Winning, losing, emerging, context-specific, contradictory |
| Scope | Platform, persona, market, source type, format, funnel stage |
| Supporting examples | At least three source IDs for repeatable patterns |
| Counterexamples | Relevant examples that do not fit |
| Baseline | Account or comparable-content reference when available |
| Observed effect | Reach, engagement, intent, objections, retention proxy, or qualitative response |
| Interpretation | Why the effect may occur; label it inferred |
| Transfer rule | What can be adapted without copying |
| Boundary | When the rule should not be applied |
| Next test | One falsifiable comparison |
| Confidence | High, medium, low |

## Synthesis Procedure

1. Group observations by hook, user moment, angle, proof, structure, product exposure, CTA, persona, and visible response.
2. Split groups by platform and source type before combining them.
3. Count independent examples and accounts.
4. Compare against baseline and negative examples.
5. Record counterexamples.
6. Write the narrowest pattern supported by the evidence.
7. Turn the interpretation into a falsifiable next test.

## Causality Guardrails

Use language such as:

- “is associated with”
- “appears repeatedly among”
- “may contribute to”
- “deserves a controlled test”

Avoid:

- “caused the post to go viral”
- “always works”
- “the algorithm prefers” without primary evidence
- attributing performance to one feature when hook, account, timing, paid support, and audience differ

## Losing-Pattern Guardrails

Do not call a post a losing pattern merely because it has fewer views. Check:

- Publication age.
- Paid or organic status when known.
- Whether the account baseline is comparable.
- Whether the content objective was conversion, education, support, or reach.
- Whether negative audience signals exist.
- Whether the same structure fails elsewhere.
